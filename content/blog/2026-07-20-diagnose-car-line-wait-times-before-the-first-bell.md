It's late July, which means principals and front-office leads are staring at a fresh calendar and thinking about the car line before it starts thinking about them. This is the right time to plan dismissal. It is also the time when most schools make the same mistake: they decide what to fix before they've measured what's broken.

We see it every August. A school had a rough spring — pickup ran long, a parent complained, someone nearly got clipped in the lot — and the leadership team walks into the fall determined to fix it. So they add a lane, or move the exit, or buy new radios, or push dismissal back ten minutes. Sometimes it works. Often it doesn't, because the change addressed a symptom nobody actually traced to a cause. Six weeks later the line is long again and everyone shrugs that dismissal is just hard.

Dismissal *is* hard. But it's also measurable, and one week of honest measurement in the first days of school will tell you more than a summer of whiteboard theorizing. Here's how to run that week.

## Measure the Curve, Not the Total

The number most schools track is the wrong one. "Dismissal took 27 minutes" is a total, and a total hides everything useful. What you want is the shape of the line over time — the curve of how many cars are waiting at each minute after the bell.

Two schools can both finish dismissal in 27 minutes and have completely different problems. One has a steep front-loaded spike: 40 cars stacked up in the first four minutes, then a smooth drain. That school's issue is arrival timing and staging — everyone shows up at once and the school can't load them fast enough at the peak. The other school has a long flat tail: the line clears fine until the last six cars, which sit for eight minutes because those students are never ready. That school's issue is readiness, not throughput. Adding a lane helps the first school and does nothing for the second.

So for one week, have someone log two timestamps per car: when it enters the queue and when the student is loaded and the car pulls away. If that's too much during the chaos, log a lighter version — a count of cars still waiting at the 5, 10, 15, and 20-minute marks. Even that coarse snapshot draws the curve. You're looking for where the line is fattest and where it refuses to drain.

> A 27-minute dismissal caused by an arrival spike and a 27-minute dismissal caused by six unready students are different problems wearing the same number. Fix them the same way and you'll fix neither.

## Find Where the Seconds Actually Go

Once you can see the curve, get specific about the dead time inside it. Dismissal delay is almost never one big thing; it's a pile of 30-to-90-second stalls that stack up behind each other. Your week of watching should catch which stalls repeat.

The usual suspects are consistent across the roughly 140 schools we work with. Students who aren't at the curb when their car arrives. A single grade that dismisses late because the class before it ran over. Parents who arrive in the wrong window and have to be re-staged. The one car that stops for a two-minute conversation and blocks everyone behind it. A sibling mismatch, where a kindergartner and a fourth grader need to leave together but arrive at the loop four minutes apart.

You don't need a stopwatch on every car to spot these. You need someone whose only job for five afternoons is to watch and write down *why the line stopped* each time it stopped. By Friday you'll have a tally, and the tally almost always surprises people. The thing everyone complained about in the spring — "too many cars" — is rarely the top line item. It's usually readiness and sequencing, which are cheap to fix, rather than volume, which is expensive.

This is also the honest way to know whether you have a throughput problem worth spending money on. If your loop genuinely cannot move the cars even when every student is ready and staged, that's a real structural constraint and worth a driveway conversation. But you should only reach that conclusion after you've ruled out the cheap stuff, not before.

## Turn One Week of Notes Into a Plan

The point of measuring isn't a spreadsheet, it's a short list of the two or three stalls that cause most of your delay. Pareto shows up reliably here: a small number of recurring causes account for the bulk of the dead time. Rank them, and you have your fall project list in priority order instead of a grab-bag of plausible-sounding changes.

Then match the fix to the cause. An arrival spike wants staggered dismissal or better staging, not a new lane. A long unready tail wants a readiness list so staff know who's not coming before the car shows up. A recurring exception — the daily two-minute conversation — wants its own space so it stops blocking everyone. Each of these is a targeted change you can defend, because you have a week of data showing it addresses your single biggest source of delay.

The schools that improve dismissal year over year aren't the ones with the most staff or the newest driveway. They're the ones that treat the car line as a process they measure, not a weather pattern they endure. Measurement is what turns "dismissal felt bad this spring" into "our line stalls in minutes eleven through nineteen because third grade dismisses late and eight students are never staged." The second sentence has a fix in it. The first one doesn't.
